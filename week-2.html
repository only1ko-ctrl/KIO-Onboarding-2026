HCSP Module

Why Healthcare Selling Is Fundamentally Different

Overview

Healthcare is not a "long sales cycle" version of enterprise SaaS. It is a categorically different environment — one where the buying decision is collective, the environment is regulated, the consequences of a wrong purchase affect human lives, and both the mission and the margin must be justified simultaneously. Reps who approach health systems the way they approached SaaS companies don't lose more slowly — they lose faster, because they visibly don't understand the world their buyers live in.

The five most consequential differences — the ones that change your approach from the first outbound touch through close:

Committee
1. The Customer Is a Committee, Not a Person
Click to flip ↩

A typical health system RTLS purchase involves 5–10 stakeholders across clinical leadership, IT, finance, supply chain, legal, and quality. No single person owns the "yes." You must map the full buying committee and earn alignment from each stakeholder in their own language. Single-threaded deals in healthcare are stuck deals.

Click to flip back ↩
Mission + Margin
2. Every Purchase Must Justify Both Mission and Margin
Click to flip ↩

Hospitals operate on razor-thin margins (often 1–3%) and are mission-driven. A CFO who can't show financial return won't approve spending regardless of clinical merit. A CNO who sees no clinical benefit will block a purchase regardless of ROI. Build a Dual Value Case — mission (patient outcomes, staff safety) and margin (ROI, TCO, payback) — and deliver both fluently.

Click to flip back ↩
Regulatory
3. The Regulatory Layer Is Constant — and an Early Qualifier
Click to flip ↩

Every technology touching a health system's network must pass HIPAA review, require a BAA, and survive an InfoSec evaluation. A 90-day security review inserted at 80% stage is how healthcare deals die. Front-load compliance: introduce the security and integration conversation in the first meeting, not the last.

Click to flip back ↩
Budget Cycles
4. Budget Cycles Are Annual, Rigid, and Capital-Planning Driven
Click to flip ↩

Most health systems plan capital budgets 12–18 months in advance. If your prospect isn't in a current cycle, the earliest realistic close could be 9–14 months away — that's not "not interested," it's a process reality. Understanding their fiscal calendar (most run October–September) and whether you're in a capital or operating budget conversation changes everything about your deal timeline.

Click to flip back ↩
Care Delivery
5. Technology Adoption Must Not Disrupt Care Delivery
Click to flip ↩

Hospitals cannot stop operating for an implementation. Every vendor pitch implying significant deployment complexity raises a "how does this affect our nurses mid-shift?" concern. Your implementation story — lightweight tag deployment, phased rollout, low IT burden, no EHR disruption — is as important as your feature story. Know it cold.

The CNO is almost always your strongest champion — emotionally invested in the clinical impact. They will advocate vigorously internally but typically cannot write the check. They need CFO and CIO alignment to close. Build them first; they open every other door.

Their Likely Objection

Your angle: Lead with the staff outcome, not the technology. Share a specific case study — "At St. Joseph's, nurses saved 22 minutes per shift on patient location searches. In the first 90 days, duress response time dropped from 4.2 minutes to under 90 seconds." Then ask: what does 22 minutes per shift per nurse mean to your retention numbers?

Health systems plan capital budgets 12–18 months in advance. How a prospect classifies your solution — hardware CapEx or SaaS OpEx — changes your entire deal timeline and structure.

Capital (CapEx) purchases — hardware, infrastructure — require board approval. Operating (OpEx) — software subscriptions, SaaS — may have lower thresholds and faster cycles. Ask early to frame your deal structure correctly.

OpEx / SaaS Path

Lower threshold, faster cycle, department-level authority possible. Subscription structure can accelerate deals significantly.

The Situation

You've had a great first call with the Director of Patient Safety at a 420-bed regional hospital. She's energized — duress response times are terrible and she's been trying to get this addressed for two years. She says: "I love what I'm seeing. But before we go any further, I need to get our CNO and our IT team involved. They'll need to be part of this."

What is your next move?

Rethink This ↩

Sending materials and going dark is how champions lose momentum. You're now relying on a non-champion to advocate internally without your support. In two weeks, this deal will be cold — and when you circle back, the CNO and IT team will have forgotten why they were supposed to care. You handed the deal back to chance.

Excellent Move ✓

Correct. You're progressing the deal while multi-threading — getting in front of IT and the CNO with your champion present, which gives you credibility and gives her control. A group call is lower friction than three separate meetings and keeps your champion engaged. Ask her to send the calendar invite — she frames you to the committee before they ever hear your voice.

Rethink This ↩

Going around your champion without her facilitation is a trust-killer. She brought you into this process — she needs to make the introduction. A cold outreach from you to her CNO before she's set the context will feel like a boundary violation and put her in an awkward position. The right ask is: "Would you be willing to make the introduction and set some context before I connect with them?"

The Setup

You're opening a 30-minute discovery call with a CNO and CIO from a 600-bed health system. You have 3 minutes to open before they start talking. Practice this out loud three times before your first real call — do not wing it.

You
"Thank you both for the time — I know your calendars don't have a lot of margin in them. "Before I dive in — I'd love to hear from both of you separately, since your perspectives are different. You've given both stakeholders an agenda they can trust, asked each person the question most relevant to their role, and gotten both talking before you've said anything about the product. You'll learn more in the next 10 minutes than most reps learn in three calls.

Talk Track — Cold Call to a Healthcare Director
The Setup

You have 90 seconds to earn 5 more minutes. The goal of this call is NOT to book a demo — it's to find out if this person has a problem worth solving. Practice out loud before dialing real contacts.

You
"Hi [Name], this is [You] from Kontakt.io — I'll be quick. We work with health systems on real-time location — tracking patients, staff, and equipment across the floor. I'm calling because [hospital system similar to theirs] was losing about $2M a year in equipment replacement costs and cut that by 60% in the first year. Is that a problem area for you, or does it live somewhere else in the organization?"
Why This Works

Discovery Arc

The 7-Step Discovery Arc

Research on discovery calls found that sellers and buyers walk away with roughly 55% misalignment on what the buyer's actual problem is. In deals that were lost, buyer and seller agreed on the problem only 23% of the time. In deals that were won, that agreement jumped to 68%. The single biggest difference between a win and a loss is almost never your pitch — it is whether you and the buyer understood the same problem. The Discovery Arc is a single repeatable sequence that closes that gap.

The Arc at a Glance — Keep This Open in a Second Tab During Every Practice Call
1 Point-of-View Open — Share researched insight before asking anything.
2 Priorities Validation & Belief Check — Confirm the real priority and how they plan to hit it.
3 Current State — Understand how they handle that priority today.
4 Obstacles & Problem Correctness Check — Find the friction, then verify it is the real problem.
5 Negative Consequences — Turn friction into business impact with "so what."
6 After State — Raise their sense of what is possible, then ask what that would look like for them.
7 Positive Business Outcomes — Define how they would measure success.

Makes the buyer do your research for you. Confirms their bias that you came unprepared.

Cross-Cutting Skill: Humanize the Call

Discovery is a conversation, not an interrogation. Lead with something specific you noticed about their hospital. Use open "how, what, why" questions. Stay flexible instead of marching through a script.

Spot the Mistake

Rep: "How many beds does your hospital have?"

That's public information. A stronger question uses that research as context: "I saw you're at roughly 300 beds and expanding your ICU this year — how is that expansion affecting how your team tracks equipment across units?"

Quick Reference — Always Running Underneath Every Step
1. POV Open: "Before I ask you anything, here's what I found..."
2. Priorities: "What did I get wrong? How would you rank this? How are you approaching it today?"
3. Current State: "Walk me through what happens today when [priority] comes up."
4. Obstacles: "Where's the friction? Is that the real driver, or is something else?"
5. Consequences: "So what happens as a result? Who else is affected?"
6. After State: Share a short story, then "What would that look like for you? What would need to change?"
7. Outcomes: "How would you know you'd solved it? What would you measure?"
Always: Listen fully → Acknowledge in your own words → Explore with a follow-up → then Respond. Lead with research, not research-able questions.

Qualification

MEDDPICC Fundamentals & Summarize Before Solving

MEDDPICC — The Qualification Checklist

MEDDPICC isn't a methodology you learn once — it's a score you update every call. If you can't answer all eight elements after two meetings, you don't have a qualified opportunity.

M — Metrics

The quantifiable economic value or ROI your solution provides — hours saved, dollars recovered, incidents reduced. No metric = no urgency = no decision.

Ask: "If this works the way you hope, how would you measure success 90 days after go-live?"

E — Economic Buyer

The specific person who has ultimate authority to release and approve funds. In health systems, this is almost never your first contact. Build your champion, then ask them to open the door.

Ask: "Who typically needs to approve an investment at this level — and have you worked with them on technology purchases before?"

D — Decision Criteria

The formal technical, operational, and financial factors the customer uses to evaluate vendors. Security posture, EHR integration, clinical evidence, implementation simplicity, TCO. Know it before you compete.

Ask: "When you evaluate vendors for something like this, what are the two or three things that matter most — and what would a clear win look like?"

D — Decision Process

The step-by-step journey the buying organization takes to make the purchase and get approvals. A champion who can't describe it hasn't been through it before.

Ask: "Walk me through what happens internally once a department decides they want to move forward — from here to signed contract, what's the longest step in that chain?"

P — Paper Process

The administrative, legal, procurement, and security reviews required to finalize contracts and purchase orders. In health systems, InfoSec review alone can add 60–90 days. Discover this before the 80% mark.

Ask: "Once you're ready to move forward, what does your legal, InfoSec, and procurement process look like — and who owns each step?"

I — Identify Pain

The critical business problems or "bleeding neck" issues your product solves. "We lose equipment" is not identified pain. "$1.2M in annual replacement costs because we have no real-time visibility" is.

Confirm: Can you state their pain in one sentence with a number? If not, keep digging.

C — Champion

A powerful, influential person within the buying organization who is invested in your success and sells on your behalf internally. A fan says "I love this." A champion says "I'll set up the meeting with the CFO."

Test: "Would you be willing to make the introduction to [Economic Buyer] and give them some context before we connect?"

C — Competition

Your knowledge of all alternative options — competing vendors, internal builds, or doing nothing. "We're not looking at anyone else" often means they're already decided elsewhere. Know the landscape before you tailor your pitch.

Ask: "Are you evaluating other options alongside us, or is this more of an exploratory conversation to understand what's possible?"

Research on complex B2B purchases finds buying committees of 6–10 stakeholders, each spending only a fraction of their time with any single vendor. The Business Case Co-Build Framework

Never hand your champion a static PDF and assume it survives a VAC room. The business case must be built together — they need to understand every number, own every argument, and defend every claim without you present. A co-built, shared document does that. A leave-behind doesn't. The difference is the champion's confidence when the CFO asks an unexpected question at minute 7.

The Four-Part Framework — Problem → Cost of Inaction → Solution → Proof
1
Problem

State the clinical or operational problem in the buyer's exact language — not vendor language. Use their metrics, their department name, their reported impact. If your champion can't repeat it verbatim, it's too abstract.

Example — 380-Bed Regional Medical Center

Our nursing staff across three med-surg units spends an estimated 45 minutes per shift per nurse searching for mobile equipment — infusion pumps, portable monitors, wound-care carts — that has been moved between units or is sitting in a hallway somewhere undocumented. We're renting $180K in supplemental rental pumps annually because we can't locate our own inventory. At the same time, our biomedical team is logging equipment as "missing" that is actually in use but untracked — creating unnecessary capital replacement requests.

2
Cost of Inaction

What happens if they do nothing for 12 more months? Quantify both dimensions: financial cost (wasted labor hours, equipment replacement loss, overtime spend) AND clinical/regulatory cost (adverse events, CMS penalty exposure, staff turnover, compliance risk). The CFO cares about dollars. The CMO cares about patient outcomes. You need both in the same document.

Example — 380-Bed Regional Medical Center

At 62 nurses across 3 units × 45 minutes of daily equipment search time × 250 working days, we're absorbing approximately 11,625 hours of non-clinical nursing labor annually — worth an estimated $520K in labor cost. The $180K rental spend is directly attributable to inventory visibility failures. The capital replacement requests generated by "missing" equipment add another $75K–$90K in unnecessary spend per year. Total annual cost of the current state: approximately $790K.

3
Solution

How specifically does your solution address the stated problem? Map each capability directly to an outcome they care about — don't list features, show outcome links. "Real-time asset location → nurses spend 0 minutes searching for infusion pumps → 45 minutes/nurse/shift recovered."

Example — 380-Bed Regional Medical Center

Kontakt.io's RTLS platform provides room-level asset location on any mobile device with no additional hardware searches required. The system integrates with our existing Epic environment via HL7, doesn't require any EHR workflow changes, and runs on a BLE infrastructure overlay that can be deployed without new cabling. Nurses locate equipment in under 30 seconds. Biomedical teams see real-time utilization rates and eliminate manual inventory audit cycles.

Proof

Evidence from comparable health systems — anonymized if necessary. Include: specific outcomes achieved, time to value, implementation timeline, and whether additional IT staff were required. The VAC will ask "who else has done this and what happened?" Your champion needs a specific, confident answer — not "I'll check with my vendor."

AE Track Champion Selling, Business Case Co-Build & MAP Execution

The Champion Conversation You're Not Having

Most reps call their champion to get a deal update. The call that actually moves deals is: "I want to help you prepare for your next internal conversation. Can we spend 30 minutes before your CFO/CMO/VAC meeting going through what they're likely to push on and how you'll respond?" The Champion Prep Session — What to Cover Every Time
1. Map the room: "Who specifically will be in the VAC meeting? What do you know about each person's concerns?" Name every attendee. Assign a likely objection to each one.
2. Scenario drill: Role-play the three most likely objections — budget, integration, and "can we wait until next year?" Make sure your champion has a confident, specific answer to each.
3. Confirm the packet is complete: Does the VAC submission have clinical evidence, ROI model, security summary, integration overview, and implementation timeline? If any of those are missing, the submission isn't ready.
4. Identify the informal veto: "Besides the formal committee, who else has real influence here — someone who could quietly kill this even if the vote goes our way?" Surface it. Plan for it.
5. Agree on next steps in writing: After every champion conversation, summarize what you each agreed to do before the next touchpoint — in the shared doc, not a reply-all email chain.

→ "If this pilot produces the results we expect, is there executive-level commitment to move to a full deployment conversation?"
→ "Who are the clinical stakeholders who need to sign off on the pilot findings — and are they briefed on what we're testing?"
→ "What specific outcome will tell us this pilot succeeded? Can we define that in writing before we start?"

CSM Track Post-Sale Stakeholder Expansion & Renewal-Stage Buyer Enablement

Churn in healthcare doesn't usually happen because the product failed. It happens because a champion left, a new CMO came in with different priorities, or a department consolidation shifted the budget — and the CSM had relationships with one person instead of a committee. This track is about building the relationship infrastructure that survives leadership turnover.

Renewal Starts at Kickoff, Not at Renewal Date

The account is most vulnerable in the first 90 days and at leadership transition moments. A CSM who only manages the primary contact is single-threaded — and single-threaded accounts are the ones that churn when that contact leaves. Your job is to build a committee-level relationship during the implementation, so the account has roots no single departure can pull up.

Post-Sale Stakeholder Expansion — Who to Build Relationships With
→ Executive sponsor: At least one QBR per year with the CMO, CNO, or department VP — not just your day-to-day champion. If you can't get on their calendar, you don't have executive access.
→ End users: Floor nurses, care coordinators, or biomedical staff who use the product daily. Their satisfaction drives clinical champion satisfaction — and their complaints surface before they become escalations if you're talking to them.
→ IT / informatics contact: The person who manages the integration should know your name. When there's a technical issue, they should call you before they call your champion to complain.
→ Supply chain / finance: When renewal comes up, these are the people who will either fast-track or slow-walk the PO. A relationship before renewal is worth 3 months of procurement friction saved.
Buyer Enablement at Renewal — The CSM's Renewal Business Case

Your renewal champion needs to make the case to their own leadership — just like in the initial sale. The CSM's job is to build the renewal business case with them, not send a renewal invoice and wait. Use the same four-part framework:

Problem: What would return if the product went away? Quantify the operational regression — the hours, the error rate, the compliance exposure.
Cost of Inaction: What was the cost of the problem before your solution? Use the actual data from the account — not a generic estimate.
Solution: What outcomes has this account actually achieved? Tie utilization data to the outcomes they told you they cared about at kickoff.
Proof: The account's own data is the proof. Pull the utilization report, the outcome metrics, the support ticket volume trend. Make it specific to their system.
Leadership Turnover Protocol

When your primary champion leaves, you have a 30-day window before the account goes cold with their replacement. Act immediately:

Week 1: Ask your secondary contacts who the transition lead or interim contact is. Get an introduction within 5 days.
Week 2: Schedule a "transition briefing" with the new leader — not a sales call, a context transfer. Bring your outcomes summary and current MAP.
Week 3–4: Re-establish the business case in the new leader's language. They may have different priorities than their predecessor — find out before renewal, not during it.

Leadership Track Coaching for Buyer Momentum — "Busyness vs. Business"

The pipeline review is the most powerful coaching moment a manager has — and most of them use it to track rep activity instead of buyer momentum. This track rebuilds the pipeline review around buyer milestones, not sales stages, and gives you a set of questions that surface stalled deals before they become stuck deals.

The Core Reframe
Busyness Coaching (What Most Managers Do)
"How many calls did you make this week?"
"What stage is this in Salesforce?"
"Did you send the follow-up email?"
"Why hasn't this moved in 30 days?"
Business Coaching (Buyer Momentum)
"Which buyer milestone is your champion actually at?"
"What does their committee need to accomplish to move forward?"
"What specifically are you doing to help them get there?"
"Who on the buying committee hasn't heard from you yet?"
Pipeline Review Questions That Surface Buyer Stalls — Not Just Rep Activity
On champion quality: "How do you know your champion has real influence and isn't just a facilitator? Have they put political capital behind this deal? What have they done to advocate internally that you can point to?"
On stakeholder coverage: "Name every person in their buying committee. Which ones have you talked to directly? Which ones haven't heard from you? What do you think the ones you haven't talked to currently believe about your solution?"
On VAC/procurement readiness: "When is the next VAC cycle? Is your champion presenting? Have you done a pre-brief with them in the last two weeks? Is the submission packet complete — clinical evidence, ROI model, security summary, integration overview?"
On buyer milestone vs. CRM stage: "You have this at 'Proposal' — which buyer milestone is it actually at? Internal Alignment? Risk Justification? What specifically does the buying committee need to accomplish to move to the next milestone — and what did you do this week to help them do it?"
On close date credibility: "Your close date is [X]. Walk me through the buyer's process from today to that date — every step they need to complete. Does that date reflect their process, or just when you need the deal?"
Exec Involvement as a Strategic Tool — When and How to Deploy It

Your involvement (or a VP's or CEO's) should be deliberate, not reactionary. As a leader, you have relationships, credibility, and access your reps don't. Deploy those strategically — not to rescue a deal that's already stalled, but to open doors that move a deal faster when timing is right.

When: Early in the deal when exec-to-exec access can compress the committee timeline. NOT as a "save" when the deal is dying at 80%.
How: The rep drafts the message. You review and send. Never a cold exec introduction — always preceded by rep-level groundwork that gives the exec something specific to reference.
Scope: Use for: opening CMO/CNO/CFO relationships, business case validation at the executive level, reference calls with health systems you have existing relationships with, and closing support when the champion has done the groundwork and needs a peer-to-peer signal.

BANT Applied to Healthcare

BANT stands for Budget, Authority, Need, and Timeline. The Kontakt.io SAO threshold is 2 of 4 criteria confirmed, with Authority and Need most often constituting the move. Neither Budget nor Timeline is a hard blocker at the SDR stage.

B — Budget

Healthcare facilities operate on annual capital planning cycles, typically opening September-November for the following fiscal year. "We do not have budget this year" almost always means "we are not in cycle," not that they can never buy.

Ask: "When does your capital planning window typically open?" Then help them build the internal case before it does.

A — Authority

Healthcare buying is committee-driven. Your goal is to find a Champion close to the decision maker who can facilitate an intro, or a decision maker directly. Relevant titles: VP/Director of Operations, CNO, CIO, Director of Clinical Informatics, VP of Supply Chain, VP of Facilities, Director of Nursing.

SAO threshold: Champion who can facilitate an intro, OR a decision maker present on the call.

N — Need

Primary Kontakt.io use cases: Staff Safety (duress alerting, fall detection, staff location) and Care Asset Management (locating infusion pumps, wheelchairs, monitoring equipment).

Need signals: high IV pump rental spend, nursing staff searching for equipment, failed prior RTLS attempt, new facility expansion, active RFP, or Epic EMR implementation (Kontakt.io integrates with Epic).

T — Timeline

No hard timeline block required. Timeline context ("We start capital planning in October" or "We are breaking ground on a new wing in Q2") strengthens deal priority.

Key point: Absence of a timeline is not a DQ reason. It feeds back into the Budget qualifier. No strict timeline block is present.

Handling the Five Most Common SDR Objections

Most objections in healthcare SDR work are not rejections — they are deflections. Your job is to stay curious, not to argue. These five responses are grounded in Jeb Blount's Fanatical Prospecting and Aaron Ross's Predictable Revenue frameworks.

"Just send me an email."

A brush-off. They expect you to hang up and disappear.

"Absolutely — I will send something right after this. Quick question before I let you go: is locating mobile equipment between units something that comes up on your floor?" If yes, you have a conversation. If no, log it and move on.

"We're happy with our current vendor."

Signals incumbent. Not a permanent no.

"That's great — who are you with, if you don't mind me asking?" Then: "How long have you been live?" If under 2 years: log and nurture. If 3+ years: "Most systems we work with at that stage are starting to evaluate whether the ROI has held up. Is that a conversation happening at [Hospital Name]?"

"We don't have budget."

Usually means "not in cycle," not "never."

"Completely understand — when does your capital planning window typically open?" Log the answer in Salesforce. Put them on a nurture cadence timed to re-engage 60 days before that window. A well-timed reconnect months later lands differently than cold outreach.

"I'm not the right person."

Can be true — or a polite exit. Find out which.

"I appreciate that — who would you say typically owns decisions around clinical workflow technology at [Hospital Name]?" If they give you a name, ask: "Would you be open to a quick introduction, or should I reach out to them directly?" Either path works. Always log the contact name in Salesforce.

"We just went live with [Competitor]."

Under 1 year: hard DQ. Move cleanly.

"That's great timing for you — congratulations on the deployment. I won't take more of your time. I'll put a note to check back in about a year when you've had some time to evaluate performance. Is there a better contact to reconnect with then?" Log implementation date and contact in Salesforce. Add to 12-month re-engage task.

Healthcare Notes Format — Required for Every SQL

Paste this structure into the Salesforce opportunity Notes field for every SQL you create:

  • Persona: [Role title of the person you spoke with]
  • RTLS: [Any mention of real-time location services or RTLS]
  • Other Tech: [Existing tools, vendors, or tech stack mentioned]
  • Current Efforts/Pains: [What they're doing now and what's not working]
  • Value Props / Use Cases: [Which Kontakt.io use cases resonated]
  • Goals: [What they want to achieve — LOS reduction, safety compliance, etc.]
  • Budget / Cycle: [Any signal on budget or fiscal year timing]
  • For Inbound Only — Ecosystem/Vertical: [How they found us, health system size/type]
When to Disqualify

Disqualify early and cleanly — a clean DQ is better than a weak SQL that wastes pipeline capacity:

  • Competitor implemented less than 1 year ago — not ready to switch; return to nurture in 12 months.
  • Budget is a real objection — not just a timing issue; no budget cycle on the horizon.
  • Authority issue — cannot get to or identify a decision-maker after multiple attempts.
  • Log DQ reason in Salesforce. Return to SDR nurture list if timing is the issue.

You start your morning with three items in your queue: an inbound form fill from a CNO at a major health system, an MQL from a Director of Operations who downloaded a whitepaper, and a batch of cold territory drops your manager assigned on Monday. What is the correct order to work these?

Inbound form fills are also urgent (24-hour SLA) but the prospect has already taken a deliberate action to reach Kontakt.io, so they will expect a response and are less likely to disappear if you respond within the day. You're 45 seconds into a cold call when the prospect says, "You know what, just send me an email." What is the best response?

"Just send me an email" is a brush-off, not a genuine end to the conversation. The correct move is a two-step: acknowledge the request so they don't hang up immediately, then deploy one short, pain-specific question to earn 30 more seconds. If they surface a pain point, you've qualified them in real time. If they don't engage, you exit cleanly and your email follow-up lands warmer than a blind cold send. Simply hanging up wastes the only live conversation you had. Pivoting to LinkedIn while they're actively dismissing you adds noise, not value.

A prospect agreed to a meeting on your call and you sent the calendar invite right after. The invite is showing as "Tentative" in your calendar. When should you create the SQL in Salesforce?

The SQL trigger is the accepted invite, not the sent invite. Sending the BOOKED MEETING email is a post-acceptance confirmation step — it does not replace the acceptance requirement. Waiting for the meeting to happen before creating the SQL is the opposite error: the SQL should exist so the AE can prepare.

Continuing to qualify wastes your time and can damage your reputation with the prospect. Re-engaging in 6 months is still too early — the ROI conversation hasn't had time to disappoint them yet. That date is when the honeymoon may be fading and the real displacement conversation becomes possible.

Territory Structure

How accounts are assigned, segmented, and prioritized — and what that means for how you spend your time.

How the Territory Works

Kontakt.io's US Healthcare territory is structured across two dimensions: segment (the size of the account) and persona (which use case is most relevant to them). Every account in your territory sits at the intersection of these two axes, and that position shapes which plays you run and how you resource the account.

Segment

  • Enterprise (IDN): Multi-facility integrated delivery networks with 500+ beds or system-wide standardization potential. Longer sales cycles, committee-driven buying, higher ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) potential.
  • SME: Standalone community hospitals and regional health systems under 500 beds. Faster cycles, more direct access to decision makers, lower ARR per deal but higher volume.

Persona

  • Staff Safety: Duress alerting, fall detection, and real-time staff location. Primary stakeholders: CNO, VP of Patient Safety, Director of Nursing, Director of Security/Public Safety.
  • Care Asset Management: Locating infusion pumps, wheelchairs, monitoring equipment, and other mobile medical devices. Primary stakeholders: VP of Supply Chain, Director of Clinical Engineering (Biomed), VP of Operations, CIO.

Monthly Account Drops

At the top of each month, Demand Generation, Marketing, and RevOps push a fresh batch of accounts into your Salesforce territory. Drops are distributed evenly by territory coverage, persona, and segment to balance workload across the team. Your job at the start of every month:

1.Review all new drops and check for DND (Do Not Disturb) status on every account before touching anything.
2.Categorize by persona and enroll each account into the correct Salesloft sequence based on their awareness level and use case.
3.Sync with your AE on which accounts overlap with active Tier 1 deals so you're not running conflicting outreach.
4.Flag any accounts you recognize from prior outreach, prior DQs, or prior close-lost opportunities — those get a different first-touch than true cold accounts.

Account Priority by Domino Tier

Every account in your territory is assigned a Domino Tier based on its market influence and deal maturity. Tier determines who owns the motion and how much of your time it warrants. Module 6 covers the full Domino Tier Playbook — but here is the orientation layer:

Key Rules

Do Not Disturb (DND)

Check the DND field in Salesforce before any outreach. If an AE has flagged an account as DND, no SDR outreach is permitted — this syncs automatically to Salesloft. Contacting a DND record is a compliance issue.

Always Existing Account

Before creating any opportunity or SQL, search for the account first. Never create a duplicate. If the account already exists under a different name variation, link to the existing record.

Prospect Outside Territory

Do not prospect outside your assigned territory without explicit manager approval. Territory boundaries are set intentionally — conflicting outreach on shared accounts creates friction, burns relationships, and poisons pipeline.

Territory Conflicts

If two reps identify an ownership conflict on an account, flag it to your manager immediately. Do not run outreach while the conflict is unresolved — two SDRs calling the same account from different territory assignments creates confusion and distrust at the prospect.

Quotes, Proposals & the SOW

The right document at the right stage — and how to complete it without creating legal or commercial risk.

The Three Closing Documents

Three documents carry the deal from SAO to Closed Won. Each has a specific trigger and a specific owner. Understanding which one to use — and when — is the difference between a smooth close and a delay caused by legal review or scope confusion.

Salesforce Quote

The commercial record of what you're selling, at what price, under what terms. Created in Salesforce and tied to the opportunity. Required before the SOW is shared.

Trigger: Quote stage (50%). AE-owned.

Proposal & SOW

The client-facing document defining scope, deliverables, timeline, responsibilities, and commercial terms. A closing instrument — never introduce it before soft commitment is in hand.

Trigger: Vendor of Choice stage (60%). AE + SE + ProServ.

MSA + BAA

The Master Service Agreement (MSA) governs the overall commercial relationship. The Business Associate Agreement (BAA) is required for any healthcare client where Kontakt.io may access Protected Health Information (PHI).

Trigger: Value Justification stage (70%). Route through legal.

Stage-Gated Document Workflow

The SOW is a closing instrument, not a discovery tool. Sending it too early creates legal exposure. Sending it too late delays revenue. Follow this stage map:

Stage % Document Action
SAO10%No SOW. Qualifying need and champion.
Discovery / Demo20%No SOW. Mapping capabilities to challenges.
Alignment30%Begin drafting SOW internally with SE & ProServ. Do not share with customer yet.
Quote50%Walk through the quote on a mutual call. Share the Salesforce quote electronically.
Vendor of Choice60%SOW is shared with the client. Awaiting vendor selection confirmation.
Value Justification70%SOW supports client's internal business case. Begin drafting MSA and BAA.
Negotiation80%Update SOW to reflect agreed quantities, pricing, and project phases.
Contracting90%Route final SOW, MSA, and BAA through Contract Signature Workflow.
Closed Won100%Signed SOW + MSA + PO + BAA (if required) in place. Handover to fulfillment.

SOW Pre-Conditions Checklist

Before opening the template, every one of these must be true. If any line is a "not yet," the SOW clock has not started.

  • Discovery is complete — you understand the client's goals, challenges, and environment
  • Solution is scoped — modules, integrations, and sites are agreed upon at a high level
  • Commercials discussed — budget, contract length, and payment structure have been addressed
  • Salesforce is updated — an opportunity exists and is accurately staged
  • Stakeholders mapped — key client contacts, including an executive sponsor, have been identified
  • Commitment received — a verbal agreement to move forward is in hand

The SOW should confirm what both parties have already aligned on — it should never be the first time a client hears about scope, price, or timeline.

What Goes Inside the SOW

Section 1: Cover + Objective
Client legal name, your contact details, solution name(s), deployment goal, key KPIs, and target population.
Section 2: Scope of Work
Services, software modules, integrations, hosting/support, key solution components, and project schedule. Check only what is explicitly confirmed in scope — delete everything else.
Section 3: Timeline + Responsibilities
Real dates (no placeholders), client responsibilities, and Kontakt.io responsibilities. Standard arc: Kickoff Week 1, Planning Weeks 1-3, Hardware Week 3-4, Deployment Weeks 4-6, Go-Live Week 7.
Section 4: Commercial Terms
Payment schedule, term, pricing, and quote notes. Commercial terms must match the active Salesforce quote exactly.
Final Check Before Sending

All [bracketed] fields filled. All highlighted fields completed. No optional items left unchecked but present — remove them entirely. Timeline reflects real agreed dates. Site table is complete with real data. Commercial terms match the active Salesforce quote. If any legal or commercial uncertainty exists, consult before sending. A partially completed SOW creates legal ambiguity and reflects poorly on Kontakt.io.

Question 1 of 4

An AE has an account at the Alignment stage (30%). A champion has verbally indicated interest and scope is roughly agreed upon. The AE wants to send the client the SOW to "start the review process early." Is this the right move?

The SOW is a closing instrument — its job is to confirm alignment, not discover it. Sharing it at 30% when no soft commitment exists invites early legal and procurement review while you're still building the business case. Once the client's legal team is in the loop, they drive the timeline — and that can add weeks or months before a soft commitment is even reached. The correct behavior at 30%: draft internally with SE and ProServ so you're ready to move fast once Vendor of Choice is confirmed at 60%. Sharing it at Negotiation (80%) is too late in the other direction — by then, scope surprises create delays right before close.

A healthcare deal reaches Closed Won. Which combination of documents must be fully signed before handing over to fulfillment?

Each document serves a distinct and non-negotiable purpose. The SOW defines scope, deliverables, and timeline. The MSA governs the overarching commercial relationship and liability framework. The PO confirms procurement approval and payment authorization from the client's finance team. The BAA is required for any client where Kontakt.io may access Protected Health Information — in US healthcare, that is effectively always required. Handing off to fulfillment before all four are signed creates downstream disputes about scope, payment, and legal authorization to handle patient data. There is no dollar threshold that removes the BAA requirement.

You're completing the SOW scope section. You're not sure whether a specific Epic integration module is in scope — the client mentioned it casually in a discovery call, but it was never formally confirmed. What should you do?

Every item checked in the SOW scope section is a commitment Kontakt.io is making. Checking something the client mentioned casually — without formal confirmation — sets up a post-signature dispute about whether it was sold. Scope disputes are the leading cause of implementation delays and client dissatisfaction. The correct process: reach out to the client to confirm the item specifically before including it. This is one email or one 5-minute call, not a negotiation. Leaving ambiguous items out and confirming them explicitly is how you protect both parties from a misaligned implementation before it starts.

Which of the following is a required pre-condition before drafting the SOW — not just completing it, but opening the template at all?

The SOW confirms alignment — it does not create it. Sending it before a verbal commitment is in hand means the client is reading a legal document that contains scope, pricing, or timeline they've never agreed to in conversation. That makes the SOW a surprise, not a summary, and triggers a reactive legal review rather than a collaborative close process. The Salesforce stage and legal readiness are downstream of this principle — they matter, but the verbal commitment comes first. If you can't say "we've agreed on scope, price, and timeline in principle," you're not ready to send the SOW.

Building a Strategic Account Plan

A living document that ties what the account cares about to what you're trying to sell — and when, and with whom.

What a SAP Is and Why It Matters

A Strategic Account Plan (SAP) is a living document maintained by the AE for every Tier 0 and Tier 1 account. It is not a CRM field — it is a written document that ties account initiatives to plays with measurable, time-boxed objectives. The difference between an AE who "knows the account" and one who has a written SAP: the written one wins more consistently, handles surprises faster, and builds multi-threaded relationships before they need them.

Mercy Health Example

Mercy Health (MO) — 5,764 beds — is a real Tier 1 account in the Kontakt.io territory. Their strategic plan captures: two prior failed RTLS pilots (technology worked, deployment model didn't), an Epic-first digital strategy, a $500K-$1M FY2026 internal budget target, and a buying committee of 11 stakeholders spanning IT, nursing, supply chain, and transformation. Without a SAP, those details live in scattered notes. With one, the entire team can navigate the account consistently across every touchpoint.

The 8 Sections of a Strategic Account Plan

1. Customer Strategy What is the account trying to achieve organizationally? What pressures are they under? What investments have they already made? This section sets the strategic context that every other section should serve.
2. Why Change? What is the account's current belief about the problem? What have they already tried? What is your reframe — the insight you bring that their current thinking misses? The best SAPs show you understand their experience better than they do.
3. Financial Posture What is the buyer's financial context? What is the internal budget target for this initiative? What ROI drivers matter most to them — cost reduction, infrastructure reuse, standardization? What makes a purchase defensible internally?
4. Risk Context What risks could kill the deal? Reputation risk, prior failed projects, competitive pressure, integration caution, internal skepticism. Name them explicitly so you can build a mitigation plan for each.
5. Stakeholder Map Every named contact: their role, their buying influence (economic buyer, technical buyer, champion, gatekeeper), their personal win, and their temperature toward Kontakt.io. A SAP without a stakeholder map is a SAP that will be ambushed.
6. Initiatives to Use Cases Map each account initiative to a measurable outcome and a specific Kontakt.io use case. This is the commercial logic of the SAP: if they care about X, the outcome is Y, and the product is Z. Every play you run should trace back to this matrix.
7. Whitespace Matrix For each use case and expansion dimension (Analytics, AI Orchestration, Geographic Expansion, Referenceability), mark Land vs. Expand. This is where the long-term revenue story lives — what you win first, and where you grow from there.
8. 90-Day Action Plan Time-boxed, goal-specific, owner-assigned. Each row has a goal, the job to be done, specific key actions and meetings, the target day range, and a status. Updated regularly — a static SAP is a dead SAP.

Competitors: Know Before You Walk In

Your SAP should document the competitive landscape for every major account. For each competitor, record why they win, why they lose, and your countermove. The Mercy Health example surfaces four: Securitas (HUGS), GE Encompass, Midmark, and Homegrown/DIY. Every Kontakt.io competitor loses for the same core reason: closed ecosystem, limited analytics, high operational burden. Your countermove is always some combination of open platform, Epic-native integration, AI-driven orchestration, and shared execution model.

How to Read the Domino Chart

The Domino chart maps every account across two axes:

Y-axis: Domino Priority Tier

Market influence of the account. Tier 0 is highest. Winning a high-tier account causes others to follow — the domino effect. An account's tier does not change based on deal stage; it is determined by strategic market weight.

X-axis: Deal Maturity

Where the deal lives today: Prospecting, Early Interest, Aligning, Mature, or Closing. The same account can move left or right on the x-axis as the deal progresses or stalls. Tier stays fixed; deal maturity moves.

Bubble color: Blue = AE owns. Orange = SDR owns. Green = Marketing owns. Purple = CS owns. Bubble size = open ARR.

The Four Tier Playbooks

Tier 0 — Expand & Retain ~13 existing customers  |  CS-owned, AE supporting

These are the 13 logos that have already bet on Kontakt.io. A happy, expanding Tier 0 account is worth more than any new logo in the territory. A churned one does damage that takes years to repair. CS owns the day-to-day relationship. AE steps in for expansion conversations, executive alignment, and commercial negotiation.

Key plays: Map the full org chart. Score each account on adoption depth, stakeholder breadth, and executive alignment. Identify the next logical expansion use case. Maintain a written account plan updated quarterly. Marketing runs always-on ABM air cover — it never goes dark on Tier 0. If churn signals appear, escalate immediately. Do not sit on risk signals.

Tier 1 — ABM: Win the Logo ~32 accounts  |  $64.1M open ARR  |  AE-owned (Mktg + SDR support)

These are the market-influencing dominoes. Win one, and others follow. AEs should spend 60-70% of their time here. Marketing ABM is always active — regardless of deal stage. The motion scales with deal maturity: Prospecting starts with multi-threaded outreach; Closing focuses on removing friction and executive alignment.

Key plays by stage: Prospecting = multi-thread + LinkedIn connection strategy + ABM air cover. Early Interest = personalized landing page + champion-building. Aligning = executive intro + internal business case support. Mature = competitive positioning + proof points from similar logos. Closing = proactive legal/procurement outreach + CS intro.

Tier 2 — Prospect & Warm ~128 accounts  |  $38.8M open ARR  |  SDR-led (AE on meetings)

Tier 2s are the pipeline behind the pipeline. When a Tier 1 domino closes, the best-warmed Tier 2 account moves up to fill the slot. SDR leads all outbound prospecting and sequence enrollment. AE steps in when a meeting is booked, an inbound arrives, or an existing relationship exists. Do not run AE cold sequences here — that time belongs to Tier 1.

Key plays: Enroll in the correct Gong Engage sequence by awareness level. Connect on LinkedIn before cold outreach. For accounts with some Kontakt awareness: lead with differentiation and ROI. Knowledge Check

Question 1 of 5

A Tier 1 account is still in the Prospecting stage — no active conversation exists yet. Should Marketing ABM be running for this account right now?

The Tier 1 green band on the Domino chart signals always-on Marketing ABM regardless of deal stage — it runs before the first SDR call, between touchpoints, and during active pipeline simultaneously. When an SDR dials a Tier 1 account cold, the prospect may have already encountered Kontakt.io content through targeted digital channels. That background warming isn't incidental — it's the strategy. It's why Tier 1 accounts have a higher first-call conversion rate. Marketing ABM at Tier 1 does not wait for SDR activation — it creates the environment that makes SDR outreach more effective.

On the Domino chart, what does the X-axis represent — and can an account's position on this axis change?

The Domino chart has two independent axes. Tier (Y-axis) reflects strategic market influence — it is fixed and does not change based on deal activity. Deal maturity (X-axis) reflects where the active conversation sits today: Prospecting, Early Interest, Aligning, Mature, or Closing. An account moves right as the deal progresses and can move left if the deal stalls, the champion leaves, or budget freezes. Understanding the independence of these axes is critical: a Tier 1 account stuck at Prospecting is still a Tier 1 account — its strategic weight hasn't changed, it just hasn't converted yet. The plays you run for that account stay Tier 1; the deal maturity column tells you which specific actions to take next.

A Tier 1 account moves to Closed Won. Which Tier 2 account should be evaluated to move into the open Tier 1 slot?

Tier promotion is about readiness, not size or geography. The best candidate for a Tier 1 slot is the Tier 2 account where the groundwork is already laid — SDR sequences have been running, the AE has existing LinkedIn connections, there's been inbound activity, and marketing has touchpoints in play. Promoting by ARR potential alone ignores pipeline reality: a warm Tier 2 account converts faster and with less friction than a large cold one. The domino effect compounds when you promote accounts that are ready to move, not just accounts that look big on paper. Geographic proximity to the closed account is irrelevant to buying committee readiness.

You're an AE with a Tier 0 existing customer. Your champion recently left the organization. What is the correct immediate action?

A champion departure in a Tier 0 account is a renewal risk event. Your champion was the person who sold Kontakt.io internally, defended the relationship from internal skeptics, and understood the value in clinical terms. When they leave, none of that institutional advocacy transfers — it disappears. Waiting 30 days means the new stakeholder's first impression of Kontakt.io is formed without you in the room. Pausing outreach entirely is even worse — it signals you only showed up when your advocate was there. Re-map stakeholders immediately, identify the new internal landscape, start building the relationship, and pull in leadership if the transition is high-risk. Renewals are won in the months before they're due, not in the renewal call itself.

A Tier 3 account starts appearing frequently in marketing engagement data — they clicked three gated content pieces, registered for a webinar, and have a VP who recently connected with your AE on LinkedIn. What does this signal — and what is the correct action?

Three gated content engagements, a webinar registration, and a VP LinkedIn connection are a multi-signal intent spike — not marketing noise. The signals are clustered and recent, which means intent is high right now. The correct response is not to jump directly to Tier 1 (that skips the Tier 2 development phase and overcommits ABM resources before the account is ready) and not to run a full cold cadence as if intent doesn't exist. The right move: flag for re-evaluation as a Tier 2 candidate immediately, activate SDR sequences while intent is warm, and let the Tier 2 playbook build the relationship before a full ABM motion is deployed. Intent signals decay — this one requires action within the week, not the next monthly account drop.
